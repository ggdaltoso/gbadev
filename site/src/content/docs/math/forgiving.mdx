## When to use it

In any platformer with jumps the player has to time. The rules of [Gravity and jumping](/math/gravity/) are exact: you can jump while standing on something, and a head that touches a block stops. Exact rules feel unfair. A player who presses A a moment after the ledge ended sees a jump that didn't happen. A head that clips a block's corner by two pixels sees a jump cut short for no visible reason. Two small lies fix both, and nobody notices them, which is the point.

[Press timing](/basics/timing/) has the other half: the jump buffer, for an A pressed a little *before* landing.

## How to think about it

**Coyote time: the ground lasts a few frames longer than the ground.** Every frame on the ground sets `coyote` to 6, and every frame off it takes one away. A jump is allowed while `coyote` is above 0, so for 6 frames after walking off a ledge the player can still jump, as if they were still on it. (The name is from the cartoon coyote who runs off a cliff and only falls once he looks down.) Jumping sets `coyote` to 0, or those same frames would allow a second jump in the air.

**Corner correction: a head that only just touches a corner slides past it.** When a rising player would move into a block, the snippet tries the same move 1, 2, 3 and 4 pixels to the left and to the right. If one of them is free, the player takes that small step sideways and keeps rising. Only when none is free does the head stop. Four pixels is small next to a 14-pixel-wide box, so a head right under a block still bumps it.

**Forgiveness is a few frames and a few pixels, as named numbers.** `coyote_frames` and `corner_nudge` sit at the top of the file with the gravity. They are part of how the game feels, like the jump speed: too small and they don't help, too big and the player can jump from thin air or slide through walls. Tune them by playing, and keep them where they can be found.

## The smallest thing that works

**←** and **→** walk, and **A** jumps. Walk off the left ledge and press A a moment late: the jump still happens. Stand under the left corner of the floating block and jump: the player slides past it and can land on top. The top line shows the coyote frames left and the last corner nudge.

Some of the C++ in the snippet:

| In the code | Why |
|---|---|
| `(x - 7).floor_integer()` | The pixel column of the box's left edge. `floor_integer` rounds down, also for negative numbers, where `integer()` would round toward 0. |
| `if(x < origin_x \|\| column >= columns) return true;` | Outside the level at the sides counts as a wall. The division would give column 0 for a few pixels left of the level, so the test is on `x`, before dividing. |
| `for(int side : { -1, 1 })` | A range-based `for` over a list written in place: left first, then right. |
| `new_y = origin_y + row * cell - 12;` | Puts the feet exactly on the top of the block that was hit, instead of somewhere inside it. |
| `hits(x, new_y + 1)` | On the ground means the pixel right under the feet is solid. |

## Pitfalls

**A jump pressed just past the edge falls.**
The snippet and a test ROM that only allows jumps on the ground got the same keys: → held from the left ledge, and A pressed a moment after the player left it. The snippet jumped and landed on the right ledge. The test ROM, at "Coyote 1", ignored A and dropped into the gap.

**Coyote time gives a second jump.**
A test ROM that didn't set `coyote` to 0 on the jump jumped again when A was pressed twice quickly from the ledge: the first jump left frames on the counter, and the second press spent them in the air. Its jump went 24 pixels higher than the snippet's with the same two presses.

**A head bumps a corner it barely touched.**
