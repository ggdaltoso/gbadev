## When to use it

In a shooter where the enemies fire more than one bullet straight down: a boss that fills the screen with rings, a turret that sweeps a spiral, a ship that throws a fan at the player. In "bullet hell" games, dodging the patterns *is* the game. Every one of those patterns is the same few lines of code. What changes is a handful of numbers, so the patterns belong in a table, and new ones are written without touching the code.

## How to think about it

**A pattern is a row of numbers.** How often it fires, how many bullets per volley, how many degrees between them, how much the whole volley turns after each one, the speed, and whether it's aimed at the player. A ring is 16 bullets 22.5° apart, which covers the circle. Make it turn 9° after each volley and fire 8 bullets, and it's a flower. Two bullets 180° apart that turn 13° are a spiral with two arms. The same loop fires all of them.

**An angle becomes a velocity: the cosine and the sine, times the speed.** At angle `a`, a bullet moves `cos(a) × speed` across and `sin(a) × speed` down each frame. The screen's y grows downward, so 90° points down and angles grow clockwise. The velocity is worked out once, when the bullet is fired, and kept in the bullet as a `bn::fixed_point`: after that it's one addition per frame. Butano's `bn::degrees_lut_cos` and `bn::degrees_lut_sin` read a table, and only accept angles from 0 to 360, so every angle goes through `wrap` first.

**A volley is bullets spread from a first angle, and aiming only moves that first angle.** The bullets of a volley are at `first`, `first + spread`, `first + 2 × spread` and so on. For a ring or a spiral, `first` is the pattern's own angle, which turns by `turn` after each volley. For an aimed pattern, `bn::degrees_atan2` gives the angle from the boss to the player, and `first` goes back by half the fan, `spread × (shots - 1) / 2`, so the middle bullet is the one at the player.

Some of the C++ in the snippet:

| In the code | Why |
|---|---|
| `constexpr pattern patterns[] = { { "Ring", 40, 16, 22.5, 0, 1, false }, ... };` | A table built at compile time, kept in ROM. Each `{ ... }` fills a `pattern`'s members in the order they're declared. |
| `sizeof(patterns) / sizeof(patterns[0])` | The number of rows: the whole array's size divided by one row's. It follows the table when rows are added. |
| `const pattern& now = patterns[current];` | A reference: a second name for that row, not a copy of it. |
| `to_ship.y().round_integer()` | `bn::degrees_atan2` takes `int`s. Whole pixels are plenty to aim. |
| `bullets.push_back({ ..., velocity })` | Fired while `! bullets.full()`: a volley that doesn't fit loses its last bullets instead of going over the sprite budget. |

## Pitfalls

**An angle past 360 stops the game.**
A test ROM that didn't wrap the angles of a volley stopped on its very first ring: the ring starts at 90° and adds 22.5° per bullet, so the 14th bullet asked for 382.5°. Butano's error screen named `degrees_lut_cos` and the angle it got, 382.5. `bn::degrees_lut_sin` and `bn::degrees_lut_cos` check their range with `BN_ASSERT`, so an angle out of range stops the game instead of reading past the table.

**A ring of 16 has 8 directions.**
A test ROM that rounded each velocity to whole pixels fired the same 16 bullets per ring, but they flew in 8 lines, two bullets on each. At speed 1, a cosine of 0.92 and one of 0.71 both round to 1, so 22.5° and 45° go the same way. The diagonals also move 1 across and 1 down each frame, about 1.41 pixels instead of 1 (worked out, not measured). Keep the velocity in `bn::fixed`.

**The aimed fan misses to one side.**
A test ROM that started the fan at the angle to the player, instead of half a fan before it, sent the first bullet at the plane and the other four off to its left. The snippet's fan, with the same keys, had its middle bullet on the plane.
