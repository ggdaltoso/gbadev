## When to use it

When a group of enemies has to move like one thing: the rows of a Space Invaders wave, a squad marching in a shoot 'em up, a line of ducks in a shooting gallery. They all step together, turn together and go down together, and the group only changes when one of them dies.

If every enemy moves on its own, with its own speed and its own path, you don't need a formation: see [Game objects](/structure/objects/) and [Velocity and acceleration](/math/velocity/).

## How to think about it

**A formation is one point, and each enemy is that point plus its cell.** The snippet keeps a single `origin`, the center of the top-left cell. An enemy only remembers its column and its row, and its place on screen is `origin + (column × 26, row × 22)`. Moving the formation is moving that one point: every enemy follows on the next step, and none of them can fall out of line.

**The edges are the outermost living enemies, not the grid.** Before each step, the snippet looks for the first and last column that still have someone in them, and checks whether the step would push one of those past the screen's edge. If it would, the formation goes down 8 pixels and turns instead of stepping. When the columns on the side die, the formation travels further before it turns, and the last enemies sweep the whole screen. The same goes for the bottom: the wave reaches the ship when its lowest living row does.

**The fewer are left, the more often the formation steps.** Each step is always 2 pixels; what changes is how many frames pass between two steps: `1 + alive / 2`. With all 28 it steps every 15 frames, 8 pixels a second. With one left it steps every frame, 120 pixels a second. Because it's a formula of the count, the curve is one line to tune.

## The smallest thing that works

Move with **←** and **→** and shoot with **A**, one shot at a time. The top of the screen shows how many enemies are left and how many frames pass between two steps. A new wave comes when they're all gone, or when the lowest one reaches the ship.

Some of the C++ in the snippet:

| In the code | Why |
|---|---|
| `constexpr int row_kinds[rows] = { 2, 1, 0, 0 };` | A table indexed by row: which kind of enemy each row has. The frame is `kind * 2 + phase`, since every kind has two frames side by side. |
| `enemy{ sprite, column, row }` | Aggregate initialization: the members in the order they're declared. See [Game objects](/structure/objects/). |
| `return 1 + alive / 2;` | Integer division rounds down, so 28 and 29 both give 15. The `1 +` keeps it at one frame or more when one enemy is left. |
| `enemies.erase(it); ... break;` | `erase` moves every enemy after `it` back one slot, so `it` and everything after it now point somewhere else. Stop right after. |
| `first_column = bn::min(first_column, alive.column);` | Starts from an impossible value (`columns`, or `-1` for the max) so the first living enemy always replaces it. |
| `phase = 1 - phase;` | Flips between 0 and 1 on every step: the two frames of the march. |

## Pitfalls

**The formation never turns, and its edge column falls off the screen.**
The first idea is often to let each enemy move itself and flip the shared direction when it touches the edge. In a test ROM written that way, the 4 enemies of the right column touched the edge on the same step, and 4 flips are no flip: that column went down a row on every step and slid off the screen, and the rest kept going right after it. With 3 enemies in that column it did turn, but only those 3 went down, and the formation tore apart. Decide once per step, for the whole formation, as the snippet does.

**The formation turns before the screen's edge.**
With the edges taken from the whole grid instead of the living enemies, a test ROM with columns 5 and 6 dead turned when the last living enemy's right edge was at x = 68, 52 pixels from the screen's edge: the two dead columns, 26 pixels each. The empty columns still hit the wall. Take the edges from the enemies that are left.

**Shooting one enemy moves the others.**
In a test ROM that placed each enemy by its index in the vector (`i % columns`, `i / columns`) instead of its own cell, erasing enemy 3 moved every enemy after it back one cell: the gap closed at once, the first bat climbed into the end of the alien row, and the hole showed up at the end of the last row. `erase` moves the elements after it, so an index isn't a place. Keep the cell in the enemy.
